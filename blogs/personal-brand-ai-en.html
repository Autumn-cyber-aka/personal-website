Why I’m Starting to Build a Personal Presence in the AI Era

As AI lowers the barrier to producing work, how can a blog help others understand the person behind it?

Contents

  2. A blog might be the fastest way to get to know someone
  3. The work you do also needs to be seen
  4. Writing is also feedback for myself
  5. How I want to begin

Lately, I’ve felt an increasing need for a place to keep track of what I’m doing, what I care about, and how I think about things. That is why I built this website and started writing this first blog post.

To put it directly, I’m beginning to think that maintaining my own public account and gradually building a personal brand is worth taking seriously.

“Personal brand” sounds a little like marketing. I haven’t fully figured out what I want mine to look like. But for now, I want people who come across my name to have something concrete to read: what I’m researching, how I approach problems, and what I’ve tried.

Is “Talk is cheap, show me your code” still enough?

There’s a familiar saying in tech: “Talk is cheap, show me your code.” Working code has often been more convincing than talking about an idea. Code, papers, and open-source projects have therefore become important ways of judging someone’s abilities.

But I think AI is changing the premise behind that saying.

When someone can use agents to quickly turn ideas into code, experiments, and complete projects, the barrier to building something keeps falling. I can even imagine ordinary people being able to bring hundreds or thousands of times their previous capacity to bear on certain tasks. Those multiples express my intuition about the scale of the change, rather than a rigorously measured result. What I mean is that the amount of work one person can accomplish may go far beyond our past experience.

This has led me to a fairly bold prediction: over the next two years, both papers and open-source projects on GitHub could rapidly lose value.

This is my prediction. More specifically, I think what may lose value fastest is the weight that “having a paper” or “building a project” carries as evidence of an individual’s ability. As these outputs become easier to produce, the output alone makes it harder to tell: how much does this person actually understand? Which important questions did they raise? Which decisions came from their own judgment?

Work that solves important problems will, of course, still have value. But understanding the person behind a piece of work may require more information than before. A polished project page, or even a complete set of experimental results, may not answer the questions I most want to ask: what kind of person are you, and how do you think?

A blog might be the fastest way to get to know someone

That is one of the main reasons I want to start blogging now.

I increasingly feel that, in this environment, a blog may be one of the best ways to help people understand someone quickly. After reading a few posts, they have a chance to see why you care about a problem, what you believe, what you disagree with, and which judgments you are willing to put forward and have challenged.

These things aren’t always easy to see in code or papers. A paper organizes its argument around a specific problem, while a project usually presents functionality that has already been built. A blog can preserve ideas that are still taking shape: a moment of hesitation, a path that went nowhere, or a judgment you later reversed. These are also part of who a person is.

Of course, AI can generate blog posts too, and writing itself will become easier to obtain. A fluent article does not automatically prove that its author thinks with the same clarity. I used AI to help organize the first draft of this post as well, then added my own views and revised it sentence by sentence.

What I want to build, then, is a record of thinking that people can keep questioning and testing. What judgment did I make today? Why did I think that? What did I do afterward? How did I revise it when new facts emerged? Readers can look at these things together and compare them with my actual actions.

For me, a personal brand develops gradually through that accumulation. People can learn not only what I’ve done, but also my interests, how I make judgments, and my limitations. Even as the tools become more powerful, these things remain worth expressing and discussing.

The work you do also needs to be seen

I’m starting to think that doing good work and helping the right people discover that work both deserve attention.

Online, people often first encounter someone through an article, a piece of code, or something they shared. Further conversation comes later. If I never express my thoughts publicly, it is hard for someone else to discover that we might care about the same problem.

An account that I keep updating can offer that entry point. After reading one piece, an interested reader can explore my earlier thoughts and learn what I’m working on. Whether it leads to making friends, discussing research, or finding collaborators, that body of writing could make conversations easier to start.

I do hope more people will see what I write. But more than the numbers on any individual post, I look forward to meeting people who want to exchange ideas. They might point out a mistake or share a direction I hadn’t considered.

Writing is also feedback for myself

Blogging has another practical value: it forces me to explain vague ideas clearly.

Something can seem perfectly clear in my head until I try to write it down. Then I discover that I can’t explain it smoothly, or that a step is missing from my reasoning. At that point, writing becomes part of learning. I need to revisit sources, organize my logic, and acknowledge what I haven’t yet understood.

AI can help me refine language, discuss an outline, or even draft a paragraph. But I still need to decide which views to keep and which claims I am willing to stand behind. If I simply accept an answer because it sounds complete, I may miss the opportunity to think for myself.

I also want this account to preserve traces of that thinking. Looking back after some time, I should be able to see which questions still trouble me and which of my views have changed.

How I want to begin

For now, I don’t want to define a fully formed public persona, and I don’t intend to wait until I feel “expert enough” to start writing.

I’ll begin with what I’m learning and trying. A question I couldn’t resolve while reading a paper, a failed attempt in a project, or an observation about the development of AI could each be the starting point for a post.

I want to be specific. Rather than vaguely saying that a direction looks promising, I want to explain what led me to that judgment. If something is a guess, I’ll say so. If I later discover I was wrong, I can return to it and add a correction.

Maintaining an account brings new temptations too. A topic becomes popular, and I want to write about it. Someone else posts frequently, and I start feeling behind. I hope I can remember that writing should grow alongside real learning and practice. If keeping up with posts crowds out the time I spend doing the work, I may have drifted away from the original purpose.

This blog is a starting point. I’ll put my ideas into words, give them a chance to be discussed, and leave a record for my future self.

I don’t yet know what it will lead to. But I’m willing to start with the first post and find out along the way.
